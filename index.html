VDC

Thousands of unworked leads became a tiered process. Response went 35% to 100%, accepted opportunities about 40% to 73% against a 60% target.

Dive Deep, Have Backbone, Earn Trust, Invent and Simplify

Glanceable
  • Situation: thousands of leads, unworkable, lost on first call, Marketing complaining
  • Task: get every lead worked and stop losing customers on the first call
  • Action
    • Raised to director, same pattern in other regions
    • Analytics swimlanes, my regional evidence, filter in the middle
    • Mine: window approach, handover rules, channel mix with EMEA and AMER
    • Analytics: time values, final design
    • Rollout across APJC, heads and country leaders, extra work for them
    • 15 min to 3 days by lead type, call, email, SMS
  • Result: 100% in 6 months, conversion nearly doubled, complaints stopped
Script

In short. I turned thousands of unworked leads into a tiered process. Response to leads went from 35% to 100% inside their set windows, and the share of Level 1 qualified leads that Level 2 accepted as real opportunities went from about 40% to 73%, against a target of 60%.

Situation. At Cisco APJC my team was handed thousands of leads from Marketing that couldn’t all be worked. We were losing customers on the first call, and Marketing kept saying leads weren’t handled.

Task. I needed every lead worked and no more customers lost on that first call.

Action. I raised it with my director, who found the same pattern in other regions. I gave Analytics the regional evidence and they identified the lead swimlanes. I pushed for a filter in the middle.

My part was the response-window approach, with Analytics setting the time values, the handover rules, and the channel mix, which I built with my EMEA and AMER counterparts. Then I shaped, influenced and led the APJC rollout, talking to business heads and country leaders first because it added work for them. Windows ran from 15 minutes to three business days depending on lead type, and we used calls, email, and SMS accordingly.

Result. Within six months every lead was worked inside its window, and Marketing stopped complaining.

VDCE

I defined what every lead had to carry before handover, then ran a monthly review showing how campaigns actually converted.

Dive Deep, Insist on the Highest Standards, Frugality, Earn Trust

Glanceable
  • Situation: no checklist, manual handoffs
  • Task: make handoffs consistent and show Marketing what actually converts
  • Action
    • Standards: date (sets priority), source, history, size, target market, what the customer saw
    • Ticket flow with completion commitments
    • Monthly heads review, conversion not impressions, budget can move before quarter end
    • Wake-up: digital-intent leads not converting
    • Cut volume, agents to warm leads, clearer vendor briefs, event changes
  • Result: Qualified Demand up 19%
  • Lesson: reduce waste
Script

In short. I defined what every lead had to carry before Marketing handed it over, then set up a monthly review that showed Marketing how their campaigns actually converted.

Situation. After the VDC went live there was still no clear checklist of what to submit or where to send it, so handoffs were manual.

Task. I needed to make the handoffs consistent and show Marketing what actually converted.

Action. I set standards for what Marketing had to capture during the event, such as when the lead was gathered, where it came from, its history, and what the customer had seen. I built a ticket process with completion commitments.

I also ran a monthly review with the heads, showing conversion instead of engagements and impressions. It showed digital-intent leads, like event business cards and freebie sign-ups, weren’t converting however we reached them. We cut how many we pursued, moved agent time to warm leads, briefed vendors more clearly, and changed how events collected contacts.

Result. In the year I remember best, Qualified Demand was 19% higher than the year before. What I took away is to reduce waste so effort goes where it matters.

If asked
  • The cut: not mine alone, budgets moved, consistent figures were their reference, contract agents became permanent
  • The cost: lower but better volume, new problem is better warm engagements

Telco

A failing 24/7 telco account, turned around. The client asked me to build a new site within six months and the team grew from 50 to 300.

Customer Obsession, Bias for Action, Dive Deep, Have Backbone, Deliver Results

Glanceable
  • Situation: day 2, 8am, agents stopped taking calls, CEO on site
  • Day 1: SLAs just short of target, missed every month, no alarm until the client was unhappy
  • Real issues: facilities, unclear measures, no incentives
  • Task: take over the failing account and get agents back on the phones
  • Action
    • Split: CEO to client CEO, me to client manager and agents
    • Mid-day update, regular updates committed
    • Motivate: used being new, asked for a chance to deliver by a committed timeframe, no change then we gather again
    • Their part: keep to schedules, theirs to own and fix, treated as managers of their own time, esp. the vocal ones
    • Realistic SLAs, trackers, dashboards, pushed back on targets
    • 90% answered, 5% abandoned max, 80/20
    • Taught rostering and call swings
    • Quality score aligned to CSAT and NPS
  • Result: failing to passing, new site, 50 to 300, client reference
Script

In short. I took over a failing 24/7 telco account on a day agents stopped taking calls, and we turned it around. Within six months the client asked me to help build a new site, and the team grew from 50 to 300.

Situation. On my second day, with my CEO on site, agents gathered at 8am and stopped picking up. The day before, I’d seen that SLAs were just short of target and missed every month, which is why nothing had set off an alarm until the client got unhappy. I read the room. The agents had real issues that had built up, such as basic facilities, unclear performance measures, and no incentives.

Task. I had to get agents back on the phones and turn the account around.

Action. My CEO took the call with the client’s CEO. I took the client’s manager and the agents. By mid-day I’d updated both and committed to regular updates until agents were back. I used being new to ask the agents for a chance to deliver what I’d promised, by a committed timeframe, and if they saw no change, we would gather again. But I needed them to do their part too, so I talked to them like managers of their own time, especially the ones who were vocal about what they wanted, and made it theirs to own and fix, not only ours, including keeping to their schedules. Then I worked with the client’s manager on realistic SLAs, set up trackers and dashboards, and pushed back on unreasonable targets. We tracked three basics, 90% answered, no more than 5% abandoned, and 80% answered in 20 seconds. I showed the team how to roster and swing calls until they could run it themselves. I also realigned the quality score to how customers measure us.

Result. The mood moved from problem-finding to solution-making in days. The account went from failing to passing, with a few hard days left, and the client now uses us as a turnaround reference.

If asked
  • How did you motivate the group: used being new, committed timeframe, no change then we gather again, made it theirs to own, treated them as managers of their own time
  • For collaboration, use the VDC story with the EMEA and AMER counterparts

A convenient decision cost me the client’s trust. I have never left a client out of a decision since.

Ownership, Earn Trust, Learn and Be Curious

Glanceable
  • Situation: new to country, dotted line to the operations director
  • Her call: target above contract as a buffer, tell client later
  • Task: my focus was fixing the floor
  • Action
    • Mine: trusted her fully, agreed, focused on the floor, convenient, did not question it
    • Client sensed it, trust issue, everything questioned
    • Fix: call floor, no director, informal trust
    • Ask: client sponsors merchandise recognition
    • TP: time off, early outs, schedule choice
  • Result: she was out of the loop, understood, saw it work
  • Lesson: never leave the client out for convenience
Script

In short. Early on, I let a convenient decision cost me the client’s trust, and I’ve never left a client out of a decision since.

Situation. I was new to Indonesia and the role, with a dotted line to the operations director. She set an agent target above the client’s contract as a buffer, so that if we fell short we might still pass the client’s targets, and she wanted to tell the client afterward.

Task. I was focused on fixing the floor.

Action. I trusted her fully and agreed with her at the time. It was convenient to let her own that while I focused on the on-the-ground fixes, so I didn’t question it. I also assumed it was how things worked locally.

The client sensed it and called it out. It became a trust issue, they leaned toward the agents over us, and everything else we presented was questioned.

Within a month I changed course. I spent time with the client on the call floor, without my director, and built trust informally. Then I asked them to sponsor recognition for agents with their branded merchandise, for most improved, no tardiness, and highest CSAT, while we added time off, early outs, and schedule choice, so neither side’s budget broke.

Result. She wasn’t there when it first came up and wasn’t happy to be out of the loop, but she saw it work. The client agreed easily because the groundwork was done.

Be ready
  • Did you tell her you disagreed? No. I trusted her fully and agreed with her at the time
  • Why was the internal target above contract? A buffer, so if we fell short we might still pass the client’s targets

Hubs

I took ten fragmented sites down to three hubs with one vendor and zero SLA breaches through cutover, and sales never stopped.

Customer Obsession, Dive Deep, Frugality, Ownership, Insist on the Highest Standards, Earn Trust

Glanceable

  • Task: coordination, efficiency, budget, sales still running

    • Diagnose: BPO eyes on waste, Six Sigma, conversion by country, trim China and India first, smaller countries cover each other
    • Redesign: keep India, China and Singapore, leader budgets for it, dashboards, tools, swimlanes
    • Vendor: easy way was keep existing, workshops pointed to one, RFP to 12, I ran evaluation and site visits, 3 months to select, 3 to launch
    • Singapore: skeptical on cost, vendor won me on languages and premium brand
    • Sales kept running: same leads, same events, categorise and prioritise, marketers and country heads in early, targets held, daily tracking

Script

In short. I took ten fragmented sites down to three hubs in China, India and Singapore, with one vendor and zero SLA breaches through cutover, while sales kept running.

Situation. I inherited marketing call centre teams in almost every APJC country, uneven in size and barely coordinated, with the same patchwork in EMEA and AMER.

Task. My job was to improve coordination, efficiency and budget use without stopping sales.

Action. I used Six Sigma tools to see which countries converted and what headcount was sustainable. I trimmed the overstaffed sites first, China and India, and had the smaller countries cover each other. That is when it made sense to change the whole approach, so I proposed keeping India, China and one more hub. My leader budgeted for it, along with new dashboards, tools and swimlanes.

The easy option on vendors was to keep the existing ones. But in our workshops it kept showing that one vendor would make the Cisco experience far more consistent. I sent an RFP to 12 vendors, some existing and some new, and led the regional evaluation and site visits. Selection took three months and the launch another three. I was skeptical about Singapore because of cost, but the winning vendor showed a compelling way to run all the languages there while keeping Cisco a premium brand.

Sales could not pause. Marketing kept running events and the same leads kept coming, so on a slimmer team I categorised and prioritised leads and focused on the types worth converting. I brought the marketers and country heads in early because it touched their numbers, held targets steady, and tracked results daily.

The hardest part was the existing agents. I was firm and fair. They still had to hit daily targets, and the performers were offered a move to the new vendor.

The near miss was the vendor wanting to run the sites and all the training themselves. I went back to why we were doing this, which was customers getting the same quality wherever they called in. So the centres sat in Cisco sites, on Cisco tools, around Cisco people, co-trained by Cisco experts.

Result. Ten sites became three hubs in China, India and Singapore, with one vendor and zero SLA breaches through cutover.

If asked
  • Why not keep the existing vendors: easy, but workshops showed one vendor gave a more consistent customer experience
  • Hardest part: the existing agents
  • Did the one vendor also cover regions beyond the three hubs: yes, one vendor across the hubs and across regions

Script

In short. I ran the sellers directly through the early launch, set quota and forecast every quarter, and qualified demand to sales averaged 73%.

Situation. I ran the sellers directly through the early launch, and a regional manager took over as it scaled. There were two layers. SDRs, a team of 10 to 15, were level one. They took the raw leads the VDC process had cleaned up and made first contact on whichever channel fit. BDRs, about 10, received validated leads, closed the deal and handed it to Partners for the actual sale. I ran against a quarterly pipeline of up to USD 50M.

Task. I set quota and forecast every quarter.

Action. I set quota at the start of each quarter from BDR headcount, using year over year, upcoming events and seasonality, which runs lower in the first half and higher in the second. I made a weekly commit with each BDR, watched some more tightly and gave performers more room, and took those numbers to leadership as the forecast. Coaching was weekly and recognition was always in place. I also took regular updates from Partners on what happened to the leads we passed.

When numbers fell short, either I knew early and told leadership in advance, or I had been overconfident on a sale that did not land. Either way I looked for where else to squeeze to recover.

Result. Qualified demand to sales averaged 73%.

If asked
  • Biggest miss: I do not recall one I did not recover before quarter end, say it that way, not as never
  • The 79% peak: likely good launch timing, do not oversell it
  • Events slipping: at most moved to the next month
  • Lead moves between reps: happened, not a heavy routine

Situation. When we launched the VDC, the first sales team sat in Singapore and covered ASEAN, New Zealand, Japan and Korea. The vendor required sales experience for every seller.
